Inspecting suspect timber

From the first look at the load to a sealed sample on its way to the laboratory. The aim is a defensible reason to hold or release, not a species name.

Red flags before you cut anything

On paper

  • Declared as an unlisted look-alike of a listed timber: merbau for doussié, "African padauk" from Asia, sapele or andiroba for mahogany, "other tropical wood"
  • Genus only, a trade name only, or a scientific name that does not exist
  • HS heading that sidesteps the annotation: squared logs as "furniture parts", sawn boards as HS 44.09, logs as "poles"
  • Origin outside the natural range, or routed through a country that does not grow the species
  • Declared value far below the market price of the species; weight that does not fit the declared wood's density
  • Permit number reused, expired, or issued for a different product, quantity or consignee

In the load

  • High-value dark or red heartwood hidden behind a facing layer of ordinary timber
  • Mixed species under one description; markings, paint codes or log tags removed or over-painted
  • Logs squared or roughly "processed" to claim a finished-product exemption
  • Wood that sinks, smells sweet or spicy, bleeds colour when wet, or shows oily black streaks
  • Fresh sawdust of a different colour from the declared wood
  • Containers from ports and exporters named in earlier seizures or CITES alerts

Kit

  • 10x hand lens, ideally lit, or a phone with a clip-on macro lens
  • New single-edge razor blades or a snap-off knife
  • Water spray bottle and a small brush
  • Millimetre scale for photographs
  • Sample bags, labels, marker, evidence seals
  • Gloves and dust mask: rosewood, cocobolo and ipê dust sensitise skin and lungs
  • Optional: 365 nm UV torch, 10% ammonia or sodium hydroxide, clear cup for water tests
  • This reference saved on your phone (it works offline after one visit)

Procedure

  1. Record the load as found

    Photograph the container or stack, seals, markings, tags and every document before anything moves. Note date, time, place, officer, declared species, HS code, origin and permit numbers.

  2. Choose the spot

    Use the end grain: the cut end of a log, plank or block. Pick sound heartwood. Avoid sapwood, stain, paint, decay and cracks. On finished goods use a hidden edge and record any mark you make.

  3. Cut a clean surface

    Hold the blade low and make one smooth slicing cut across about one square centimetre. Repeat until the surface shines. Sawn and sanded surfaces hide the pores. Then wet it lightly.

  4. Read the end grain in a fixed order

    Pores present or not. Pore pattern across the ring. Pore size and number. Pore contents. Pale tissue around the pores. Rays. Then turn to the side face for ripple marks. In softwoods look for resin canals. Use the same words as the 10x key.

  5. Note what the hand tells you

    Heartwood and sapwood colour, weight, smell of the fresh cut, oily feel.

  6. Compare

    Run the features through the key, open the candidate profiles and compare with the photographs. Read the look-alikes for each candidate.

  7. Decide

    Features fit a listed timber and documents are missing or do not match: hold and send samples. Features do not fit the declared species: treat as mis-declaration. Check the annotation and population before you conclude that a document is required.

  8. Photograph and sample

    Photograph the cut surface dry and wet with the scale in view, then take samples as set out below.

Sampling and chain of custody

What to take

  • Wood anatomy: a heartwood block about 2 x 2 x 5 cm, or a sliver 5 cm long
  • DNA: clean heartwood shavings drilled into a new bag, away from treated or weathered surfaces. Change blade and gloves between samples.
  • Mass spectrometry or infrared: matchstick-sized heartwood splinters
  • Sample enough pieces to represent the load: every distinct colour, size class and marking, and pieces from the middle of the stack as well as the outside
  • Keep a duplicate of every sample

Custody

  • One bag per sample, sealed, with case number, sample number, date, place and officer
  • Mark the sampled piece with the same number and photograph it in place
  • Log every transfer: who, when, why, seal number intact
  • Store dry and cool. Never use plastic for wet wood going to DNA analysis: use paper
  • Tell the laboratory the declared species and the suspected species, and ask which question they can answer

What a laboratory can tell you

MethodUsually answersLimits
Wood anatomy (microscope)Genus, sometimes a species groupRarely separates species within Dalbergia, Pterocarpus, Swietenia, Cedrela
DART-TOF mass spectrometrySpecies within a genus, where reference spectra existNeeds heartwood and a reference library for the species
DNA barcoding and genetic markersSpecies, and for some timbers the region of originDNA is degraded in old, heated or treated wood
Stable isotopesWhether the declared region of harvest is plausibleNeeds reference maps for that species
Near-infrared spectroscopyFast screening between known speciesDepends on calibration with verified samples

Finding a laboratory: Global Timber Tracking Network. Identification manuals and keys: CITES timber identification repository. Forensic standards: UNODC Best Practice Guide for Forensic Timber Identification (2016).

Adding your own reference photographs

Photographs from verified specimens (xylarium samples, laboratory-confirmed seizures) appear first on each profile. Only add images you own or that carry an open licence.

  1. Cut or sand the end grain of confirmed heartwood. Field of view about 5 to 7 mm at 10x.
  2. Even light from a low angle, no glare. Take one dry and one wet. Include a millimetre scale. Keep the rays vertical.
  3. Upload the file to photos/<taxon-id>/ in the repository and add a row to photos/manifest.csv with magnification, specimen source and number, photographer and licence.
  4. The site rebuilds itself and shows the photograph on the profile.
