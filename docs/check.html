Five checks, in this order

  1. Species

    Is the wood a listed taxon? Compare the declared name with what the end grain shows. A declared look-alike is the commonest disguise.

  2. Population

    Does the listing cover wood from this origin? Khaya, Afzelia and Pterocarpus: Africa only. Cedrela and Swietenia macrophylla: Neotropics only. Diospyros: Madagascar only. Appendix III: every origin needs a document, but which one depends on the country.

  3. Product form

    What is physically in the container, by HS heading? Logs, sawn wood, veneer, plywood, transformed wood, chips, extracts or finished goods.

  4. Annotation

    Does the annotation cover that form? No annotation means everything is covered.

  5. Documents

    Is the right permit or certificate present, genuine, in date, and does it match species, quantity, origin and consignee?

National law can be stricter. The EU, for example, requires an import permit for Appendix II (Annex B) timber and an import notification for Appendix III (Annex C). Timber-legality laws apply on top of CITES.

Check the permit in front of you

  • Original document, or a copy clearly marked as a copy where a copy is allowed
  • Issuing Management Authority is real: name, stamp and layout match known specimens; security stamp and unique control number present
  • Issued within the last six months and not expired (import permits: up to twelve months)
  • Scientific name and Appendix match the goods and the customs declaration. A permit for a look-alike species is a red flag.
  • Quantity and units (m³, kg, pieces) match the load; nothing above the permitted amount; part-shipments endorsed
  • Country of origin, exporter and consignee match the transport documents. A re-export certificate must refer to the original export permit.
  • Source and purpose codes are plausible for the goods
  • The product description matches a form the annotation covers
  • Not already used: a cancelled permit cannot cover a second consignment
  • No alterations or erasures unless stamped and signed by the issuing authority
  • Electronic permit verified in the issuing country's system or with its Management Authority, not from a printout alone
  • Any export quota for the species and year has not been exceeded (quota number on the permit)

If something does not match: hold the goods, photograph the permit and markings, take wood samples (how) and ask your national CITES Management Authority to verify with the issuing country. Contacts for every Party: CITES national authorities directory.

Exemptions: verify, never assume

  • Transit and transhipment under customs control need no CITES documents. Check that the goods really stay under control.
  • Pre-Convention specimens need a pre-Convention certificate from the exporting or re-exporting Management Authority.
  • Personal and household effects are often exempt, with exceptions for Appendix I and for items acquired abroad. Countries set their own thresholds.
  • Scientific exchange between registered institutions travels under the authority's label.
  • Annotation exemptions (10 kg under #15, finished musical instruments, retail-packaged products) take the product out of control altogether. Read the full text on Species+.
